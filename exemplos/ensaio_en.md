# On Time

Time does not run; it is we who pass through it, slowly, like someone crossing a river of calm water. We call past whatever we have left on the shore, and future whatever we have not yet seen on the other side. Between the two banks there is only the gesture of walking, and it is there that life takes place. Whoever mistakes the clock for time ends up measuring haste rather than duration.

There are moments that stretch out without anyone asking: a rainy afternoon, a conversation with no destination, the interval between a question and its answer. In those moments we notice that time is not a straight line but a breath. It contracts when we are afraid and widens when we pay attention. Attention, then, is the true unit of measure of an existence.

Perhaps that is why the ancients spoke of time with reverence. They did not treat it as an enemy, but as a silent teacher who instructs without raising his voice. Learning from it demands patience, that small and stubborn virtue people only value after they have lost it. To wait is not to waste time; it is to grant whatever is about to be born the delay it needs.

# On Silence

Silence is not the absence of sound but the presence of listening. When everyone falls quiet, the room remains full: of breathing, of ideas that have not yet found their words, of memories waiting for their turn. That is why silence troubles those who fear their own thoughts, and welcomes those who have learned to live with them.

Words that are born from silence carry a different weight. They lose the easy shine of habit and return to their origin, like water going back to its spring. A sentence spoken after a long pause sounds truer, because the pause works as a filter: what cannot withstand silence hardly deserved to be said.

Some seek silence in distant places, in the mountains or in monasteries. But it also lives in small things: in the first hour of the morning, in the gap between two pages, in the instant when the rain stops and the whole street seems to hold its breath. It is enough to stop for a moment and hear it arrive, without haste, like the night.

# On Waiting

Waiting is a less passive art than it seems. The farmer who waits for the harvest does not sit with folded arms: he tends the soil, watches the sky, learns to read the signs. True waiting is made of care, and care turns the interval into part of the work rather than an obstacle to it.

We live in an age that despises the interval. We want the answer before the question has ripened, the result before the effort is done. Yet what is essential tends to arrive slowly. Friendship, understanding, a well-made craft: none of these is obtained by shortcuts, and those who try to hurry them realize, too late, that they were running in the wrong direction.

In the end, perhaps wisdom consists in accepting the rhythm of things. Not the rhythm we desire, but the one they have. Whoever learns to wait discovers that the pause also speaks, that time works in our favor once we stop fighting it, and that every ending is only the silence before a new beginning.
